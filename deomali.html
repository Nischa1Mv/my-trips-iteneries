Vizag → Deomali

Two-day motorcycle run to Odisha's highest peak, up through Araku and Balda, night on the summit, home on NH26.

Working notes. Timings are estimates, not a schedule — the only hard rule is arriving on Deomali in daylight.

Non-negotiables

Through the Deomali gate before 18:00. The district administration bars night entry. Late means turned away, and your booking doesn't help you.

Off the ghats before dark. Free-roaming cattle, no lighting, blind hairpins, trucks. Locals don't ride these roads at night.

Don't swim in Balda Pond. It looks shallow and isn't. People have drowned there.

Bail-out rule. Not at Balda by 11:00? Skip it and ride for Deomali. The booked night matters more.

Balda

Balda village, via Padwa, Nandapur block, Koraput. Flat tabletop summit with a pond, views over Jalaput reservoir, and trains crossing the valley below. The cave itself is small — the plateau is the reason to go.

Deomali

The camps sit on the flat plateau just below the summit, with vehicle access right up to the parking zone beside the camping ground — you ride to your tent and carry nothing. The summit itself is a separate 15 to 30 minute walk from there on a clear paved path, up to the watchtower. The plateau runs about 5 km across.

Tents ₹500 a head, campfire ₹300 for the group. Two-wheeler parking runs ₹30–50 a bike and foot entry about ₹30 a head, charged at the checkpoints — walking in is otherwise free. Bring the whole lot in cash: UPI regularly fails at the hilltop on the thin network.

Six of you — three bikes, two up

  • Book the tents now. It's a small camp on a popular peak. Six spots on a weekend is a big ask at short notice, and the fallback is riding back down to Semiliguda in the dark.
  • Balda's last 2 km is the pinch point. Reviews say a single rider gets up that off-road stretch and two-up doesn't. So the three riders take the bikes up light and the three pillions walk the 2 km. Decide that at the bottom, not halfway. Budget half an hour for it.
  • Six people through one gate before 18:00. Stragglers don't get a second chance — anyone not through is spending the night in Semiliguda alone.
  • Agree regroup points before you leave. Araku, Balda junction, Semiliguda, Doodhari. No phone signal for most of the day, so nobody waits at the roadside wondering.
  • Lead and sweep. Slowest rider sets the pace and rides second; most experienced rides last. Six bikes strung out over a ghat is how people get separated.
  • One puncture kit isn't enough for six bikes. Two, plus a pump, plus one person who's actually used them.
  • Water is per head. Six people and no source on the summit — that's a lot of bottles to carry up from the parking point.

Food

No raw chicken from Vizag. Sixteen hours unrefrigerated on a bike in September is the exact route to the bathroom situation you're avoiding — on a mountain with no toilets. Buy it at Semiliguda instead.

Ask the camp first

There's a dining hall up there. If dinner and breakfast are available, the whole problem disappears and you carry snacks only. Settle this when you book — it changes what you pack.

Check what the ₹300 campfire actually is

A paid campfire is often an ambience service — they light it, you sit around it. That's not the same as a cooking fire with usable embers. Ask whether wood is included, whether it's lit early enough to cook on, and whether they mind you putting food in it. If it's decorative only, plan food that needs no fire at all.

The plan: boiling water does the work

Everything below needs hot water or embers. Nothing needs a pan, oil or skill, and nothing sits heavy before a four-hour ride.

Dinner, in order

  • Soup sachets first. Tomato or sweetcorn, one each, the moment you arrive cold. Warms you while the rest happens.
  • Potatoes into the embers. Wrapped in foil, buried once the fire settles, out in about 40 minutes. Sweet potatoes and corn too.
  • Instant khichdi cups, or retort dal. Pour or submerge, wait five minutes. Khichdi is what you eat when your stomach is fragile — that is exactly the point here.
  • Theplas or rotis from home to go with it. Two or three a head. They keep for days and don't crush.

Chicken, bought at Semiliguda

Buy it there around midday, not in Vizag. That's roughly six hours to the fire instead of sixteen, and the last few of those are cold.

  • Boneless, cut small, and ask the shop to cut it. Small pieces cook through fast, which is the whole safety question with chicken on a fire.
  • Marinate the moment you buy it. Tip your masala straight into the bag, squeeze the air out, knead it. The bag is your marinating bowl and your carrier.
  • Carry a frozen bottle of water against it. Freeze a 1 l bottle the night before, pack it alongside the chicken in the middle of a bag. It keeps the meat cold for the climb and you drink it later.
  • Cook in foil packets, not on skewers. Chicken, sliced onion, a spoon of oil, sealed tight, into the embers for 20–25 minutes. Nothing drips into the fire, nothing chars on the outside while raw inside, and you need no grill.
  • Cut one open before serving. No pink. There's no argument to be had about this at 1,672 m with no toilets.

Ask the camp too — some will grill it for you, which saves the whole business.

Masala box from home

One small container, pre-mixed, so there's nothing to measure in the dark: salt, red chilli, turmeric, garam masala or tandoori masala, black pepper. Add a small bottle of oil, a lemon or two, and ginger-garlic paste in a tiny jar. Salt does double duty on the potatoes.

Kit

A pot for boiling, one mug and spoon each, plenty of foil, tongs or a long stick, a knife, zip bags for the marinade, a frozen water bottle, and a rubbish bag. That's it — everything else is eaten out of its own cup, which matters because there's no spare water for washing up.

Ask whether the camp supplies hot water. If it does, drop the pot and this gets much simpler.

Buy the water at Semiliguda. 18 litres is 18 kg. Don't carry it 273 km when there's a shop an hour below camp.

Leave behind

Curd and raita, cut fruit, anything deep-fried, cup noodles as a meal, and roadside food on the ride up. Eat plain and boring on day one — the toilets are the reason, and there aren't any.

Pack out everything you take up, including peels and foil.

Medkit

One proper kit on one bike, plus a pouch on each of the other two with bandages, antiseptic, painkillers and ORS. If the group splits on the ghat, nobody should be empty-handed.

Road rash and cuts — the likely one

  • Sterile gauze pads, a roller bandage and a crepe bandage
  • Povidone-iodine solution, antibiotic ointment, assorted plasters
  • A bottle of sterile saline to wash grit out of a wound — plain bottled water if not
  • Micropore tape, scissors, tweezers, two pairs of gloves
  • A triangular bandage, which doubles as a sling

Stomach

  • ORS sachets, six or more. The single most useful thing in the kit. Rehydration matters more than stopping anything.
  • Antacid, and something for nausea
  • An anti-diarrhoeal for getting someone down the mountain — not a fix, and not the right call if there's fever

Everything else

  • Paracetamol and ibuprofen — the second also handles the ache after 273 km
  • Muscle spray or gel
  • An antihistamine for bites and reactions
  • Burn gel or aloe, for the fire
  • Lubricating eye drops — dust and insects at speed
  • Instant cold pack, thermometer
  • Mosquito repellent. Koraput is a malaria belt; cover up at dusk and again at dawn.
  • Hand sanitiser, wet wipes and toilet paper. There are no toilets up there and this is not the place to improvise.
  • Lip balm and moisturiser — cold, wind and 1,672 m will crack you open

Everyone brings their own prescriptions, doubled, and tells one other person where they are. Inhalers, insulin, anything for a known allergy. No network up there means no phoning a pharmacy.

Ambulance is 108 in both Odisha and Andhra Pradesh. Nearest real hospitals are at Koraput and Sunabeda, both well below the peak.

At 1,672 m you don't need anything for altitude — that's a hill, not thin air. Cold is the thing that will actually bother you.

Parked ideas

Galigabadar falls

11 km from Pottangi at Deomali's foothills, open 7:00–18:00. The easiest add-on if you take NH26 up instead of Araku.

Kalyamali — the peak you actually walk up

Odisha's fourth-highest at 1,548 m. Ride to the last village, then 1.5 km on foot, 30–45 minutes, to a summit ringed by hills with a cliff on the fourth side. Needs a third day and local directions.

Thuria Konda

1,597 m, about 90 minutes of trekking, with a view across to Deomali. The hardest walk of the three.

Rani Duduma falls

26 km from Semiliguda via Nandapur. Swimmable, changing room, ₹10 entry and ₹20 bike parking. Roughly 700 m walk from the car park. Best in winter — monsoon flow makes the rocks dangerous.

Araku loop on the way home

Instead of NH26 both ways: come down via Sunki to Araku, then Borra, Anantagiri and S. Kota. Adds 30–40 km and an hour. Day 2 becomes about 270 km.

Quick stops near Semiliguda

Maliguda — India's highest broad-gauge railway station and tunnel, ten minutes. Kunduli — big weekly tribal market, worth timing the trip around; ask locally which day. Talamali — quiet hills, same dawn fog as Deomali without the crowd.

Skipped: Red Mud Pond, Damanjodi

Caustic bauxite waste on active NALCO land. The drone shots were flown, not walked. Not a visit.

Skipped: Duduma (Machkund) falls

Different waterfall from Rani Duduma, about 90 km from Koraput, and needs formal permission. Its own trip.
